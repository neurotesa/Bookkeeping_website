Part 1 · Data check

Before counting, I checked the data

1

Checked every formula in the profit column

Profit = Gross Receipt − COGs. One row had a hardcoded 1 instead of the formula (true profit $30.16), two rows pointed to the next row. Fixed and highlighted.

2

Fixed a miscategorized line

A Gelato vape was filed under the category "Accountants" and the brand "Alex". The other 13 lines of the same product are Vape Cartridges / Gelato.

3

Converted text to numbers

Quantity Sold was stored as text ("2.0 ea"). A helper column turns it into numbers with VALUE(SUBSTITUTE()).

4

Flagged what needs the owner's confirmation

102 lines with no region, 154 accessories with no brand, 4 lines on Hold in a Completed report, a delivery fee recorded as a product.

Part 1 · Findings

What the numbers say

Two categories bring two thirds of profit

Flowers (37.4%) and Vape Cartridges (30.1%). Accessories have the highest margin, 91.9%, but bring less than 1% of profit.

One brand leads

Gelato earned $43.4K, 12.7% of profit, about twice the next brand. The 10 weakest brands sold 1–3 units each in the month.

Sales team

The top 5 of 39 employees bring 27.7% of profit. Two employees have margins of 61% and 54% against a 70% average and earn $15 and $12 per line against $26. Their discounts should be reviewed.

Regions and days

Walnut Creek, Concord and Bag and Carry drive 62% of sales. The two best days were Sundays, Oct 6 and Oct 13: weekends are worth planning staff and stock around.

Note: "Profit" as defined in the data (Gross Receipt − COGs) includes excise, city and sales taxes and delivery fees, which are not the company's income. I showed Net Sales − COGs next to it as the more accurate gross margin: 59.7% instead of 70.3%.

Part 2 · Transactions of the year

From journal entries to a year-end forecast

1

Receivables on Net 30

$1M of sales: 85% collected on time, 7% after 60 days, 4% after 90 days, 4% uncollectable. Allowance method: bad debt expense in January, write-off on April 1.

2

Equipment bought with a loan

$500K equipment, 15% down, $425K loan at 1.5% a month. Amortization table with PMT: 10 payments of $46,084.53, $35,845 of interest. Straight-line depreciation at 12.5% a year.

3

Inventory and cycle count

2,500 units for $800K, half sold at a 35% markup ($540K). 50 missing and 2 defective units written off. Ending inventory of $383,360 ties to the account.

4

Prepaid insurance

$120K policy from July 1: $10K expense a month, $60K still prepaid at Dec 31.

5

Year-end forecast

Monthly P&L, cash flow, A/R and A/P and the balance sheet at Dec 31, built from all the transactions above with 1% monthly sales growth.

Part 2 · Forecast

Can the company repay a $2.15M note on Dec 31? No.

The year is profitable: $13.2M revenue and $2.62M net income, a 19.8% net margin. But only $1.35M is left in cash on Dec 31, about $795K short.

The difference between profit and cash is tied up in the balance sheet: $288K in receivables, $383K in unsold inventory, $533K in equipment and CapEx, $60K in prepaid insurance.

Judgment calls

Where I had to make a call

Bad debts hit the P&L once

The expense is estimated in January, against January sales. The write-off on April 1 moves the balance from the Allowance against A/R and does not touch the P&L again.

Missing inputs as assumptions

No opening cash or balance sheet was given. I assumed zero and put every assumption in input cells, so the whole forecast updates when an input changes.

A/P is zero

COGS and payroll are paid in the same month, inventory and insurance in cash. The equipment loan and the note are loans, not Accounts Payable.
